Australian Consumer Law (ACL) — Key Rights

Consumer guarantees apply automatically to goods and services sold in Australia and cannot be excluded or reduced by store policies or “no refunds” statements.

  • If a product has a major problem, you can choose a refund or replacement.
  • If a product has a minor problem, the business must repair it for free. If it can’t be repaired in a reasonable time, you can choose a refund or replacement.
  • If a service has a major problem, you can cancel the service and choose a refund or replacement where applicable.
  • Businesses must not tell consumers to go to a manufacturer for a remedy. The seller is responsible for resolving the issue.
  • Change‑of‑mind returns are not required under the ACL, but if a business offers a change‑of‑mind policy, it must follow it.
  • Refunds should be provided in the same form as the original payment unless the customer agrees otherwise.
  • Consumer guarantees are separate from warranties. Warranties are extra promises and do not remove ACL rights.

Before Escalating a Complaint

The first step is to contact the business that sold the product or service to explain the issue and the outcome you want. Many issues can be resolved quickly at this step.

Access Canberra (ACT Consumer Help)

Access Canberra can help with consumer issues when:

  • You live in the ACT (regardless of where the business is in Australia).
  • You do not live in the ACT but have a dispute with an ACT agent, motor vehicle dealer or motor vehicle repairer.

Access Canberra can provide consumer advice and help resolve disputes, but cannot give legal advice, order compensation, or require a business to fix a problem — only a court or tribunal can do that.

ACAT (ACT Civil & Administrative Tribunal)

ACAT is an independent tribunal in the ACT. It hears civil disputes, including claims about contracts, debt, goods and the Australian Consumer Law. ACAT can hear civil claims up to $25,000 and provides procedures intended to be simple and accessible. ACAT staff can provide procedural information but not legal advice.

Privacy (Privacy Act 1988 & APPs)

The Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) regulates how personal information is handled. It applies to Australian Government agencies and private sector organisations with an annual turnover of more than $3 million, as well as some other organisations. The Act includes 13 Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) that cover collection, use, disclosure, and security of personal information.

The Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme requires certain organisations to notify the OAIC and affected individuals if a data breach is likely to cause serious harm.

Marketing Communications (Spam Act 2003)

Commercial electronic messages must include a clear unsubscribe option. Unsubscribe requests must be actioned within 5 working days, be free of charge, and remain functional for at least 30 days after the message is sent.
